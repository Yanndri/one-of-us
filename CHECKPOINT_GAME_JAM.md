## Theme and purpose

The theme is **Play to Prevent**. The jam concerns preventing violent extremism:
recognizing and countering pathways to radicalization to violence through
pro-social behavior, digital resilience, empathy, and inclusive communities.
It is not simply a game about reaching checkpoints.

Sources: [official website](https://checkpointgamejam.org/),
[jam page](https://itch.io/jam/checkpoint-game-jam).

## Judging priorities

The linked rubric lists these category maxima:

| Category | Points |
| --- | ---: |
| Prototype & Gameplay | 30 |
| Theme Integration | 30 |
| Creativity & Innovation | 20 |
| Cultural Relevance | 20 |
| Overall Experience & Execution | 10 |

These published values add to 110. Preserve them as points; do not silently
convert them into percentages or assume a 100-point total.

Theme integration emphasizes player decisions and consequences, meaningful
prevention mechanics, empathy, and constructive alternatives. Dialogue alone
is insufficient for strong integration. Cultural context should be credible,
respectful, and nuanced; avoid stereotypes or simplistic accounts of extremism.
Gameplay should be understandable, engaging, balanced, and technically stable.

## Registration and submission

- Each participant must register individually, not just the team leader.
- At least half the team must be citizens or residents of Indonesia, Malaysia,
  the Philippines, or Singapore. Participants must be 18+ by 31 October 2026.
- Registration form deadline: 31 October 2026.
- Jam submission closes 31 October 2026 at 11:59 p.m. Anywhere on Earth
  (1 November at 7:59 p.m. in the Philippines). Check the live portal before submitting.
- For consideration for the two-month stage, submit the game, a concept/initial
  game design document, and a two-minute video showing actual submitted gameplay.
- AI use is permitted but must be disclosed. Development assistance and generated
  asset variants have been used in this project; prepare an accurate disclosure.

## Application to One Of Us

Design interpretation, not an organizer requirement: the rhythm game explores
conformity, shaming, and exclusion. Friend defending a newcomer is a beginning;
letting the player actively support someone else could make inclusion playable.
Avoid suggesting that exclusion inevitably causes violent extremism.

These are considerations for future design discussions, not authorization to
add mechanics. Andrew prefers small, explicit changes he can follow and edit.
